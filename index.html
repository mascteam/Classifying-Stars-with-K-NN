Part 1 · The Stars
1

What is a star?

Every pinpoint of light in the night sky is a giant sphere of hot gas making its own light.

Real lifePicture a campfire across a dark field: from a kilometre away it shrinks to a tiny pinprick, yet you can still tell whether it is a roaring blaze or a fading red ember.

A star is a huge ball of mostly hydrogen and helium gas held together by its own pull of weight (gravity). Deep inside its core, the gas is squeezed so tightly and heated so strongly that small atomic nuclei slam together and fuse into heavier ones (nuclear fusion), releasing a steady flood of light and heat.

Because stars sit trillions of kilometres away, even the largest ones look like sharp dots to our eyes. Yet if you look closely on a clear night, those dots are not identical: some shine with a crisp blue-white tint, others glow warm yellow like our Sun, and many shimmer a soft orange-red.

RememberA star is a giant ball of hot gas that makes its own light, and its colour and brightness carry clues about what is happening on its surface.

2

Hot things glow

The colour of a glowing object tells you its temperature before you ever touch a thermometer.

Real lifeSwitch on an electric stove ring or watch a blacksmith's iron bar: as it heats up, it glows dull red first, then bright orange, and finally a dazzling yellowish-white.

Any dense object that is hot glows across a smooth spread of wavelengths (blackbody radiation). Notice that everyday art labels reverse the physics: we call red "warm" and blue "cool" in paint, but in physics a blue flame is much hotter than a red ember.

As temperature rises, two things happen at once: every part of the surface shines much brighter, and the peak colour shifts toward shorter, bluer wavelengths (Wien's displacement law). Scientists measure surface temperature on an absolute scale starting at the coldest possible state (kelvin, K), where room temperature is about 300 K and the Sun's surface is about 5,772 K.

For the curious: Planck's curve and Wien's peak formula

Max Planck showed in 1900 that the light intensity per unit wavelength from an ideal glowing surface at temperature \(T\) follows:

$$B(\lambda, T) = \frac{2hc^2}{\lambda^5 \, \left(e^{\frac{hc}{\lambda k_B T}} - 1\right)}$$

Finding where this curve reaches its highest point gives Wien's law: the peak wavelength in nanometres is inversely proportional to temperature in kelvin:

$$\lambda_{\text{peak}} \approx \frac{2{,}897{,}772\text{ nm}\cdot\text{K}}{T}$$

Check yourself: If Star A looks orange-red and Star B looks blue-white, which star has the hotter surface?

Star B is much hotter. A blue-white glow peaks at shorter wavelengths, which requires a surface temperature above about 10,000 K, whereas an orange-red star sits below about 4,000 K.

RememberHotter stars glow brighter at every wavelength and shift their peak light toward shorter, bluer wavelengths.

3

Star colours and classes (O B A F G K M)

Astronomers sort stars from hottest to coolest using seven classic letters and a digit from 0 to 9.

Real lifeThink of spice-heat labels on a restaurant menu—from mild to extra fiery—except astronomers named the groups before they knew temperature was the true ordering rule.

When nineteenth-century astronomers first sorted starlight, they labelled spectra A, B, C, and so on by the strength of hydrogen lines. Decades later, physicist Cecilia Payne-Gaposchkin and Annie Jump Cannon showed that the sequence actually tracks surface temperature (spectral class). Once rearranged from hottest to coolest, the surviving letters spell O B A F G K M.

Generations of students remember the order with the classic phrase "Oh Be A Fine Girl/Guy, Kiss Me" (or "Only Boring Astronomers Find Gratification Knowing Mnemonics"). Inside each letter, subclasses run from 0 (hottest in that letter) down to 9 (coolest in that letter): our Sun sits at about 5,772 K, making it a G2 star.

4

Barcodes in starlight

Optional

Dark vertical lines carved out of a star's rainbow tell us exactly which chemical elements float in its outer atmosphere.

Real lifeAt a supermarket checkout, a scanner reads the spacing of dark bars on a product label to know whether you are buying tea or biscuits.

When light from a star's hot, dense core travels outward, it starts as a smooth, unbroken rainbow (continuous spectrum). Before escaping into space, that light must pass through the star's cooler, thinner outer gas layer (photosphere and chromosphere).

Inside atoms, electrons cannot float at random heights; they sit on fixed energy rungs (quantised energy levels). An atom only swallows a photon of light if that photon carries the exact energy needed to boost an electron from one rung to another, leaving a sharp dark gap in the rainbow (absorption line). Because every element—hydrogen, helium, sodium, calcium, magnesium, iron—has its own ladder of rungs, each stamps a unique barcode onto starlight.

5

Bright because big, or because hot?

A cool star can still outshine a blazing hot star if its surface area is gigantic.

Real lifeA tiny blowtorch flame is much hotter than a classroom radiator, yet a huge radiator warms a winter room far more because its surface area is thousands of times larger.

When astronomers talk about a star's true total power output (intrinsic luminosity), they measure how much energy the entire star pours into space every second, not how bright it happens to look from Earth. Two dials control that total power: how much light each square metre of surface emits, and how many square metres the star has.

Heating a star's surface makes each patch shine dramatically harder, while puffing out its radius gives it a vast surface area. That is why the red supergiant Betelgeuse (cool at about 3,600 K, but about 760 times the Sun's radius) outshines our Sun by roughly a hundred thousand times, while the white dwarf Sirius B (blistering hot at about 25,000 K, yet smaller than Earth at about 0.008 solar radii) glows at only about 2% of the Sun's total brightness.

For the curious: The Stefan–Boltzmann luminosity law

A sphere of radius \(R\) has surface area \(4\pi R^2\), and each unit area radiates power \(\sigma T^4\). Dividing by the Sun's values (\(R_\odot\), \(T_\odot = 5{,}772\text{ K}\)) cancels the constants:

$$\frac{L}{L_\odot} = \left(\frac{R}{R_\odot}\right)^2 \left(\frac{T}{5{,}772\text{ K}}\right)^4$$

For Betelgeuse (\(R \approx 760\,R_\odot, T \approx 3{,}600\text{ K}\)), \(760^2 \times (3600/5772)^4 \approx 8.7 \times 10^4 \approx 10^5\) times the Sun. For Sirius B (\(R \approx 0.008\,R_\odot, T \approx 25{,}000\text{ K}\)), \(0.008^2 \times (25000/5772)^4 \approx 0.022\) times the Sun.

RememberA star's total brightness depends on both its surface temperature and its size, so a giant cool star can easily outshine a tiny hot one.

6

The HR diagram

Plot brightness against temperature and stars do not scatter at random—they gather into distinct neighbourhoods.

Real lifeIf you plot the height and weight of every animal at a veterinary clinic, creatures naturally group into kittens, adult cats, medium dogs, and giant horses rather than filling the chart evenly.

Around 1910, Ejnar Hertzsprung and Henry Norris Russell plotted stars on a two-axis map (the Hertzsprung–Russell or HR diagram). By tradition, astronomers put surface temperature along the bottom with hot stars on the left and cool stars on the right, and true brightness along the vertical axis measured as standard distance brightness (absolute magnitude, Mv).

Watch out for a historical quirk on the vertical scale: ancient Greek astronomers ranked the brightest naked-eye stars as "first magnitude" (1) and faint ones as "sixth magnitude" (6). Modern astronomy kept that backwards numbering, so smaller and more negative magnitude numbers mean brighter stars (a star at −10 is vastly brighter than a star at +15).

When we plot our 240 real stars below, they fall into clear families: a long diagonal band of stable hydrogen-burning stars (main sequence), cool faint embers at the bottom right (red dwarfs and brown dwarfs), hot tiny cinders at the bottom left (white dwarfs), and swollen luminous beacons across the top (supergiants and hypergiants).

Watch outBoth axes on an HR diagram run backwards compared to everyday graphs: moving left means hotter, and moving up (toward negative numbers) means brighter.

What are AI and ML?

Instead of writing a thousand rigid if-else rules by hand, we let the computer learn patterns from examples.

Real lifeNobody taught you to recognize your best friend's voice by handing you a sheet of sound-wave equations; you heard their voice dozens of times until your brain learned the pattern.

When a computer performs tasks that usually need human intelligence—such as translating speech, playing chess, or sorting starlight—we call that broad field smart computing (Artificial Intelligence, AI). Inside AI sits a practical approach where the computer improves by studying real data rather than following hand-typed rules (Machine Learning, ML).

Most beginner machine-learning problems fall into two styles depending on whether the training data comes with answer labels:

Learning style What the computer gets Everyday examples
Learning with answer labels (Supervised learning) Examples paired with the right answer label (such as 240 stars already tagged with types 0 to 5). Voice-to-text captions on your phone, predicting flat prices, sorting spam emails.
Finding groups without labels (Unsupervised learning) Raw data points with no tags; the computer discovers which items naturally cluster together. Amazon or Flipkart "customers also bought" groups, grouping listeners by music taste.

RememberMachine learning learns patterns from examples instead of hand-written rules, using labelled answers in supervised learning or finding natural groups in unsupervised learning.

Data has a shape

Turn every measurement column into a map axis, and each row in a spreadsheet becomes a point in space.

Real lifeAt a fruit stall, you can judge a piece of fruit by weighing it in your hand, tasting how sweet it is, and hearing how loudly it crunches when you bite.

In machine learning, each measurable property of an item—like a fruit's weight or a star's temperature—is called a measurement column (feature). Each individual item in your table is a single data row (sample or point).

If we measure two features (weight and sweetness), we can plot every fruit on a flat 2D sheet. Add a third feature (crunchiness) and the points float inside a 3D box you can turn with your hand. In geometric space, real data forms recognisable shapes: parallel stripes (bands), a circle surrounded by a loop (rings), or separate pockets (islands). As you will see in Tool 6, a single straight dividing line (linear classifier) works on two clean groups, but fails on stripes, rings, and multi-pocket islands.

How close is "close"?

Before a computer can find a point's nearest neighbours, we have to choose a ruler for measuring distance.

Real lifeA crow flying straight across an open park, a taxi driving along square city blocks, and a chess king stepping across a board all measure the trip from A to B differently.

When we ask which training points sit closest to a new point, the answer depends on our distance rule (distance metric). Here are the three classic rulers used in machine learning:

Straight-line distance (Euclidean): Walking directly across an open field in a straight line. The points at distance r from A form a smooth circle.

Grid-walk distance (Manhattan / taxicab): A chess king restricted to moving only up, down, left, or right along grid lines. The points at distance r form a tilted diamond.

Biggest-step distance (Chebyshev / chessboard king): A regular chess king that may step in any direction including diagonals. Because a diagonal step covers 1 horizontal and 1 vertical square at the cost of a single move, only the larger coordinate gap counts, and points at distance r form a flat square.

For the curious: The three distance formulas

For two points \(A = (x_1, y_1)\) and \(B = (x_2, y_2)\) with horizontal gap \(\Delta x = |x_1 - x_2|\) and vertical gap \(\Delta y = |y_1 - y_2|\):

$$\text{Euclidean } (L_2): \quad d = \sqrt{\Delta x^2 + \Delta y^2}$$

$$\text{Manhattan } (L_1): \quad d = \Delta x + \Delta y$$

$$\text{Chebyshev } (L_\infty): \quad d = \max(\Delta x, \Delta y)$$

RememberHow "close" two points are depends on your ruler: straight-line Euclidean distance draws a circle, grid-walk Manhattan draws a diamond, and king-move Chebyshev draws a square.

10

K-NN: ask your neighbours

To label a brand-new point, find its K closest neighbours and hold a majority vote.

Real lifeIf you walk into a stadium without knowing which team sits in your section, looking at the jerseys of the 5 people sitting closest to you will almost always tell you which side's stand you are in.

The K-Nearest Neighbours rule (K-NN algorithm) is one of the friendliest ideas in machine learning because it does no complicated algebra up front. Instead, it stores the known training points right on the map.

When a mystery point arrives, K-NN follows three calm steps: 1. Measure the distance from the mystery point to every known point. 2. Pick the K closest points (where K is a small odd number like 3 or 5 so two-way votes don't tie). 3. Count their labels and give the mystery point whichever family wins the majority vote.

Check yourself: Why do we usually pick an odd number for K (like 3, 5, or 7) when deciding between two groups?

To prevent tied votes. With two groups, an odd number of neighbours cannot split 50–50, so one family is guaranteed to win by at least one vote.

RememberK-Nearest Neighbours labels a new point by finding its K closest training points and taking their majority vote.

How is K decided?

Optional

Pick K too small and the computer memorises noisy mistakes; pick K too large and it blurs real shapes away.

Real lifeK = 1 trusts a single rumour from the nearest person in the hallway. A huge K asks the whole school and always gets the biggest group's answer.

Real-world datasets are rarely spotless—a few points sit in the wrong place or carry a flipped label (noise). If we set K = 1, every single training point carves out its own tiny territory. On the points it already knows (training set), K = 1 scores a perfect 100%, because every point is its own closest neighbour! Yet on fresh, unseen points (test set), K = 1 gets tricked by noisy outliers—a trap called memorising noise (overfitting).

On the other hand, if we set K = 49, the neighbourhood stretches across almost the entire map, drowning out smaller clusters in favour of the biggest crowd—a trap called blurring away structure (underfitting). By holding back 30% of our data as a fair test set, we can slide K from 1 to 49 and pick the "just right" sweet spot where test accuracy peaks.

Classifying a mystery star

The HR diagram is a machine-learning data space—drop an unknown star onto it and let its neighbours name its family.

Real lifeWhen a new student transfers into college, seeing which lab, club room, and lecture hall they spend their day in tells you their department right away.

Look back at the HR diagram from Block 6 with machine-learning eyes: it is a 2D feature space! Each of our 240 known stars is a labelled training point, and the six stellar families (0 Brown dwarf through 5 Hypergiant) are the classes.

One crucial detail before we measure distances: temperature runs up to 40,000 K while absolute magnitude runs from −12 to +20. If we plugged raw numbers into our distance ruler, a small 500 K change in temperature would completely swamp a huge 10-magnitude change in brightness! To keep the vote fair, we first put both axes on an equal footing by measuring how many typical spreads each value sits from the average (standardised z-scores on log₁₀ temperature and Mv).

Recap and take-home

Fourteen small steps took us from a glowing campfire to a working star classifier.

Real lifeBefore you close this page, think of one thing a computer predicts or recommends in your daily life—and ask yourself what "features" and "neighbours" it might be comparing behind the scenes.

Block Core idea in one line
1 · What is a star?A star is a giant ball of hot gas making its own light, whose colour and brightness carry physical clues.
2 · Hot things glowHotter objects shine brighter and peak at shorter, bluer wavelengths, while broad emission blends green peaks into white.
3 · Star colours & classesStars are sorted from hottest to coolest as O, B, A, F, G, K, M with subclasses 0–9 (the Sun is about G2).
4 · Barcodes in starlightCooler outer gas absorbs exact wavelengths, stamping each chemical element's unique fingerprint onto starlight.
5 · Bright: big or hot?Total brightness depends on both surface temperature and surface area, so huge cool stars can outshine tiny hot ones.
6 · The HR diagramPlotting brightness against temperature reveals that stars gather into distinct families rather than scattering randomly.
7 · What are AI and ML?Machine learning learns patterns from examples—using labelled answers (supervised) or discovering natural clusters (unsupervised).
8 · Data has a shapeMeasurement columns become axes and rows become points, forming bands, rings, and islands that a single straight line cannot split.
9 · How close is "close"?Euclidean (straight line), Manhattan (grid walk), and Chebyshev (chess king) metrics draw circular, diamond, and square neighbourhoods.
10 · K-NN: ask neighboursK-Nearest Neighbours labels an unknown point by taking a majority vote among its K closest training points.
11 · How is K decided?Tiny K memorises noise (overfitting) and huge K blurs groups away (underfitting), so we pick K using held-out test accuracy.
12 · Classify a mystery starStandardising HR diagram coordinates lets K-NN classify unknown stars by voting across their nearest stellar neighbours.
13 · Code it yourselfSplitting with stratification, scaling with StandardScaler, and fitting K-NN (K=3) reaches 96.67% accuracy on the 240-star dataset.
14 · Recap & take-homePhysics tells us which features matter, and machine learning uses geometry in feature space to sort the night sky.

RememberWhen you combine domain science (which features describe a star) with geometric machine learning (who sits nearby in feature space), even a simple neighbour vote can sort the night sky.
